5 questions to ask before hiring an AI consultant

May 1, 2026

Hiring an AI consultant is a bet. You’re paying someone to tell you what to build, and often to build it. If they get it wrong, you lose time, money, and sometimes your team’s willingness to try automation again.

I’ve been on both sides of this, and the pattern is consistent: the projects that fail almost always had warning signs at the hiring stage. Here are five questions that would have caught most of them.

1. What does your discovery process look like?

This is the most telling question you can ask. A good consultant will describe a structured process: mapping the current workflow, interviewing end users, and defining success criteria before proposing a solution.

A bad sign is any version of “we’ll figure it out as we go” or jumping straight to a demo. If they lead with the solution before understanding the problem, they’re selling, not consulting.

2. Can you show me a project where you recommended against AI?

This separates consultants who solve problems from consultants who sell AI. Anyone who works in this space long enough will have projects where the right answer was a script, a better process, or nothing at all. If they’ve never talked a client out of AI, they’re either too new or too incentivized to recommend it regardless.

The goal is finding someone who treats AI as one option among several and picks the right tool for the problem.

3. What happens when the system you build gets something wrong?

Every automated system fails at some point. The question is whether your consultant has thought about it.

A good answer describes error handling and what the end user sees when something needs manual review. It also addresses how the system behaves with unexpected inputs.

A bad answer is vague reassurance that the system is accurate. Accuracy rates are meaningless without context. 95% accuracy sounds good until you realize that on a process handling sensitive IP, that's a 5% chance of leaking it. At scale, those odds stop being theoretical very quickly!

4. Who on my team will you need to talk to?

If the answer is only “your CTO” or “your project sponsor,” that’s a problem. The people who use the tool daily have a different understanding of the process than the people who approve the budget. Both perspectives matter, but the end user perspective is the one that determines whether the tool gets adopted.

A consultant who only wants to talk to leadership is optimizing for the sale, not the outcome. The ones who ask to sit with the people doing the work are the ones who build things that actually get used.

5. How will we know this project succeeded?

The answer should be specific and tied to a business outcome. “Reduce audit prep from 10 days to 3.” “Eliminate the need for temporary hires during peak cycles.” “Cut manual data entry by 80%.”

If the success metric is “deploy the model” or “launch the platform,” that’s a technology milestone, not a business outcome. You can deploy a model that nobody uses. You can launch a platform that makes the process worse. The metric needs to reflect what changes for the people doing the work.

What these questions have in common

They’re all getting at the same thing: does this consultant care more about solving your problem or selling their solution?

The consultants who do good work will welcome these questions because they already think this way. The ones who get defensive or vague are telling you something about how the engagement will go.

Note: These questions work just as well for evaluating internal AI projects. If your own team can’t answer them clearly, the project isn’t ready to start. Discovery comes first!
